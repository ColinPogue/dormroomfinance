*I'm not a financial advisor, just a finance student sharing what I've actually done and learned. Do your own research before making any financial decisions.*

My first internship paycheck had $340 withheld in taxes I hadn't accounted for. I'd built out this whole mental budget based on my gross salary, and then the actual deposit landed and it was just... less. Not catastrophic, but enough to throw off every number I'd been running. I spent an hour that night actually reading about FICA and federal withholding, and honestly, that was one of the more useful hours of my entire summer.

The point isn't that taxes are surprising. The point is that most people's first instinct when that deposit hits is to spend, and if your numbers are already off because you planned against gross income, you're starting in a hole before you've bought anything.

So before you do anything else, understand what you actually took home. That's the only number that matters.

## The Tax Situation Nobody Explains to You Beforehand

When you get paid as an intern, your employer withholds federal income tax, Social Security at 6.2%, and Medicare at 1.45%. That FICA piece alone is 7.65% off the top, every single paycheck, no exceptions. If your internship pays $25 per hour and you're working 40 hours a week, your gross is $1,000 per week but your take-home is going to be somewhere around $820 to $850 depending on your federal withholding elections and your state.

New York takes another bite on top of that. When I was interning here, state and city taxes combined knocked off another 7 to 9 percent depending on the bracket. That's real money.

Before you make any decisions about where this paycheck goes, check what you actually received versus what you expected. If you haven't looked at your W-4 elections yet, now is the time. You can also check out [how to file taxes as a college student for the first time](/posts/how-to-file-taxes-as-a-college-student-for-the-first-time/) to understand how withholding now connects to what you'll owe or get back in April.

One thing that trips people up is assuming they'll get a refund. Maybe you will. But that's not free money, it's money you overpaid and got returned without interest. Getting the withholding roughly right is smarter than treating a tax refund like a bonus.

## What to Actually Do With the Money

Here's what I did the first month of my internship: I moved $500 to my Roth IRA at Fidelity before I touched anything else. Treated it like a fixed bill. Whatever was left after that and after rent was mine to do what I wanted with. That framing matters because it removes the temptation to negotiate with yourself every single month.

A lot of people talk about budgeting as this complicated allocation exercise. I think it's simpler than that. You have three buckets for every paycheck from an internship: obligations, savings, and the rest. Get the first two out of the way immediately and you can spend the third without guilt.

Obligations are rent, utilities, transit, food. If you're in a city like New York or San Francisco, this number is going to be high. My first summer here, rent plus groceries plus transit was around $2,500 out of roughly $3,800 take-home. That left $1,300 for everything else, including the Roth contribution. It's tight but it's workable, and knowing the actual numbers before you arrive makes the whole thing less stressful.

Savings means two things at your age. An emergency fund if you don't have one, and a Roth IRA contribution if you do. Those are not the same goal and they shouldn't come from the same account.

For the emergency fund, I keep mine in a Marcus by Goldman Sachs high yield savings account, currently paying 4.50% APY with no minimum balance and no monthly fees. The account has no frills but that's fine because an emergency fund isn't supposed to be exciting. It's supposed to sit there and compound quietly while you don't touch it. SoFi and Ally are both decent alternatives at similar rates, but Marcus is what I've used for two years without any issues.

For the Roth, I use Fidelity. No account minimums, no fees, and I invest in FSKAX, which is Fidelity's total market index fund with an expense ratio of 0.015%. That number is not a typo. For every $10,000 you have invested, you're paying $1.50 per year in fees. Vanguard's VTSAX is a comparable option at 0.04% if you already have an account there, but Fidelity's zero minimum makes it easier to get started when you're contributing small amounts.

The 2026 Roth IRA contribution limit is $7,000, but you can only contribute up to what you actually earned from working. If your internship pays you $6,000 over the summer, you can put in up to $6,000. Most people won't max it out in one summer, and that's fine. Something is always better than nothing, and the earlier you get money into a Roth, the longer it compounds tax-free.

For more context on how much of each paycheck to actually save, I wrote about this more specifically in [how much to save from your first job paycheck](/posts/how-much-to-save-from-first-job-paycheck/).

## The Credit Card Thing You Should Not Ignore

An internship paycheck is usually the first documented income you have, which means it's also the first time you're a genuinely attractive applicant to credit card issuers. If your credit file is thin right now, that matters.

I want to be specific here because vague advice about credit cards is useless.

My daily driver right now is the Chase Freedom Flex. No annual fee, 5% cash back on rotating quarterly categories (which you do have to activate, so set a reminder), 3% on dining and drugstores, and 1% on everything else. The sign-up bonus is typically $200 cash back after spending $500 in the first three months, which isn't hard to hit in a city. The variable APR runs from 19.99% to 28.74% depending on creditworthiness, but if you're paying the balance in full every month, that number is irrelevant.

If you're newer to credit, the Discover it Student Cash Back card is worth looking at. It's what I started with before I had the history to get the Chase card. No annual fee, 5% cash back on rotating categories up to $1,500 per quarter, and Discover matches all the cash back you earn in your first year automatically. So if you earn $150 in cash back your first year, they double it to $300. The APR range is 18.24% to 27.24% variable, but again, pay it off monthly and that number means nothing.

What you want to avoid is the trap of treating a credit card like it extends your budget. It doesn't. It's a payment tool that earns you rewards when you use it for spending you were going to do anyway.

## Building a Habit Instead of Making a One-Time Decision

The most useful thing about your first internship paycheck isn't the dollar amount. It's that it's the first real data point for understanding your own financial behavior under real conditions.

I could be wrong on this, but I think the people who build strong financial habits in their twenties are mostly just people who made the boring decisions early and consistently. They didn't find some secret investment. They set up automatic contributions, kept their fixed expenses lower than their income, and didn't overcomplicate it.

Your internship is probably 10 to 12 weeks. If you take home $3,500 per month and contribute $500 to a Roth, keep $2,000 for living expenses, and put $500 into savings, you end the summer with $1,000 in savings, $1,500 in Roth contributions, and a paycheck-by-paycheck habit that actually sticks. That's not a dramatic number. But it compounds, and more importantly, you'll know you can do it, which matters more than you'd think when you're doing it on a full salary after graduation.

The first paycheck is just a starting point. Don't overthink it. Do something deliberate with it, and the rest gets easier.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do I have to pay taxes on my internship income?**
Yes. Internship income is regular earned income and subject to federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare withholding, plus any applicable state and local taxes.

**Q: Can I contribute to a Roth IRA from internship income?**
Yes, as long as you have earned income. You can contribute up to the amount you earned from working, with a maximum of $7,000 for 2026, whichever is lower.

**Q: What's the difference between a high yield savings account and a regular savings account?**
A regular savings account at a big bank typically pays 0.01% APY or less. A high yield savings account like Marcus by Goldman Sachs currently pays 4.50% APY, which on a $3,000 emergency fund is roughly $135 per year versus almost nothing.

**Q: Should I start investing before I have a full emergency fund?**
At least in my experience, it makes sense to have one to two months of expenses saved before you start investing, then build both simultaneously once you have that floor in place.

**Q: Is it worth opening a Roth IRA as an intern if I can only contribute a small amount?**
Yes, because the account age matters for certain Roth rules, and because getting into the habit of contributing is more valuable long-term than waiting until you can contribute more.
