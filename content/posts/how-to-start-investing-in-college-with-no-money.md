I'm not a financial advisor, just a finance student sharing what I've actually done and learned. Do your own research before making any financial decisions.

"No money" is doing a lot of work in that phrase. Most people who say they have no money to invest mean they have no *extra* money after spending freely. That's a different problem than actually being broke, and it has a different solution. If you're a college student with any kind of income at all, even $200 a month from a part-time job or a summer internship, you have more to work with than you think.

I opened my Roth IRA at 19 with the first paycheck that actually felt like real money. Put $400 into FSKAX at Fidelity. It took about 20 minutes to set up and was completely anticlimactic. That was the point. I'm not telling you that to sound impressive. I'm telling you because the bar to start is genuinely that low, and most people don't realize it until someone just says it plainly.

## What "Starting" Actually Requires

You need three things to start investing: earned income, a brokerage account, and something to buy. That's it.

Earned income means money from a job, a 1099 gig, or a paid internship. It does not include money gifted to you or student loan refunds. This matters specifically if you want to open a Roth IRA, which I'd argue is the single best account available to a college student. The contribution limit in 2026 is $7,000, but you can only contribute up to what you actually earned. If you made $2,400 this year, that's your ceiling.

A brokerage account is just where you hold investments. Fidelity has no account minimum, no recurring fees, and lets you buy fractional shares of index funds starting at $1. Schwab and Vanguard are also solid options, but Fidelity is where I'd point someone starting from zero. I've written more about the differences at [Fidelity vs. Vanguard for Beginners](/posts/fidelity-vs-vanguard-for-beginners/) if you want to dig into that comparison. The short version: for most college students, Fidelity wins on accessibility.

Once your account exists, you need something to buy. For most people starting out, a total market index fund is the answer. FSKAX is Fidelity's version. It holds thousands of US companies, charges an expense ratio of 0.015%, and requires no minimum investment. You're not going to beat it by picking individual stocks. I learned that the hard way freshman year, bought a company I thought I understood, and sold at a loss eight months later. Index funds since, no regrets.

## The Roth IRA Is Built for This Exact Situation

Every finance professor I've had eventually says some version of "the Roth IRA is the most powerful account for young investors." They're right, and I don't say that because it sounds good. The math actually works out that way.

Here's why it matters so much in college specifically. You're almost certainly in a low income tax bracket right now, maybe 12% or even 10% if your earnings are modest. The Roth IRA lets you contribute after-tax dollars, meaning you pay taxes now at that low rate and then never again on the growth. When you're 45 and that account has compounded for 25 years, you owe nothing on any of it. Compare that to a traditional 401k or IRA where you defer taxes and pay them later at whatever rate applies to your peak earning years. The college years are genuinely one of the best times to be loading a Roth, not because you have more money, but because your tax rate will probably never be lower.

The contribution rules are worth knowing before you move money. In 2026 you can put in up to $7,000, or your total earned income for the year if that's less. Contributions can be made up until tax day the following April, so a summer internship you worked in 2026 is eligible until April 15, 2027. That extra runway matters when you're trying to scrape together contributions around a class schedule.

## Finding the Money When You Think There Isn't Any

This is where most people get stuck, and honestly it's less about income than about sequence. The first month of my internship, I moved $500 into my Roth IRA before I touched anything else. I treated it like a fixed bill. Whatever was left was mine to spend. That single habit change did more than any budgeting spreadsheet I've ever made.

The math people don't run: if you invest $150 a month starting at 19 and earn an average of 8% annually, you'll have roughly $970,000 by 65. If you wait until 25 to start, same amount, same return, you end up with about $600,000. That $370,000 difference came entirely from six years of $150 monthly contributions, which is $10,800 of actual money. That's compounding. It is not magic. It's just time, and college students have more of it than anyone.

Some concrete places the money can come from without requiring a dramatic lifestyle change. A bank maintenance fee you're currently paying is just money leaving your account for nothing. I got hit with a $12 monthly maintenance fee sophomore year and switched accounts the same week. That's $144 a year that now earns 4.5% APY in my Marcus by Goldman Sachs high yield savings account instead of going nowhere. Put your emergency fund in Marcus and redirect what you were paying in fees toward investments. That's not a sacrifice, that's just paying attention.

If you have a credit card, make sure it's actually earning something. I use the Chase Freedom Flex as my daily driver. It has no annual fee, currently offers a $200 cash back bonus after spending $500 in the first three months, and earns 5% on rotating quarterly categories plus 3% on dining and drugstores. The variable APR runs from 19.99% to 28.99%, so you carry zero balance. The cash back goes straight to my brokerage contribution. It's not a huge amount but it's real money that would otherwise just disappear into normal spending.

Small income sources also add up faster than people expect. A single paid tutoring session, one freelance project, or a few hours of gig work can cover a full month's investment contribution. The goal in college isn't to invest thousands at once. It's to build the habit and put in whatever you can consistently. A $50 month is infinitely better than a $0 month.

## What Not to Do

I'll keep this short because the mistakes aren't complicated, they're just common.

Don't wait until you have "enough" to start. There is no threshold. Fidelity lets you invest with $1. Waiting for a round number or a perfect moment is how you lose years.

Don't open a taxable brokerage account before you've maxed your Roth IRA for the year. A taxable account is great, but you're throwing away tax-free growth by prioritizing it over the Roth when you haven't hit the limit yet. The order matters: Roth first, taxable after.

Don't pick individual stocks because you follow a company or like their products. I know this from personal experience. The confidence that comes from being a customer is not the same as understanding a business well enough to bet money on it. FSKAX and similar total market funds exist precisely because most individual stock pickers, including professionals, underperform over time.

Don't let the interface make the decision for you. Robinhood is designed to feel like a game. That's not accidental. I'd rather use something less polished that isn't built to maximize how often I open the app. Fidelity isn't exciting. That's a feature.

Starting small is not the same as starting weak. The account I opened at 19 with $400 has grown into something that genuinely matters to my financial picture, not because I contributed a lot, but because I started before I had any real reason to.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can I open a Roth IRA if I only made money babysitting or doing odd jobs?**
Yes, as long as the income is earned and you report it, cash income from informal work counts toward your eligible contribution amount.

**Q: Do I need to invest every month or can I just invest once a year?**
You can contribute in a lump sum anytime before the tax deadline, but investing monthly tends to work better in practice because it keeps the habit active and takes advantage of dollar cost averaging across different price points.

**Q: What if I have student loan debt? Should I invest or pay that off first?**
Federal student loan interest rates are generally low enough that investing in a Roth IRA while making minimum loan payments makes mathematical sense, but high interest private loans above 7% or 8% probably deserve payoff priority before you invest aggressively.

**Q: Is a Roth IRA the same as a brokerage account?**
No. A Roth IRA is a tax-advantaged retirement account held at a brokerage. A regular brokerage account has no contribution limits or tax benefits. You can have both, and eventually you probably should.

**Q: What happens to my Roth IRA if I need the money before retirement?**
You can withdraw your contributions (not earnings) at any time without penalty. Withdrawing the earnings before age 59½ generally triggers taxes and a 10% penalty, with some exceptions, which is why it's worth treating it as money you won't touch.
