I'm not a financial advisor, just a finance student sharing what I've actually done and learned. Do your own research before making any financial decisions.

Credit card debt in college hits different than other kinds of debt. It's not like student loans where you can defer it and pretend it doesn't exist. It's sitting there every month, accruing interest, quietly getting worse while you're trying to study for midterms. The average credit card APR right now is somewhere around 27%. That's not a typo. That's real money disappearing for every month you carry a balance.

If you're in it right now, I'm not here to lecture you about how you got there. I'm here to tell you how to get out.

## Understand What You're Actually Dealing With

Before you can pay anything down, you need to know exactly what you owe. Not approximately. Exactly.

Log into every account and write down the balance, the APR, and the minimum payment for each card. If you only have one card, this takes three minutes. If you have two or three, it still shouldn't take more than fifteen. The point is to stop letting the number live in the back of your head as some vague source of dread and put it on paper where it can't scare you as much.

Here's what you're up against on a typical student card. The Discover it Student Cash Back card, which was actually my first card, runs a variable APR between 18.24% and 27.24% after the intro period. The Capital One Quicksilver Student card is similar, sitting around 19.99% to 29.99% variable. These aren't outliers. This is just what unsecured credit costs when you have a limited credit history, and understanding [how credit scores work](/posts/how-credit-scores-work-explained-simply/) helps explain why issuers price it that way.

If you're carrying a $1,500 balance at 24% APR and only making the $35 minimum payment each month, you're going to be paying that off for years. The math is genuinely brutal. Run it through any credit card payoff calculator if you want to feel sufficiently motivated.

## The Payoff Strategy That Actually Works

There are two main approaches people talk about: the avalanche method and the snowball method. The avalanche method means you pay off the highest APR card first, which is mathematically optimal. The snowball method means you pay off the smallest balance first, which gives you psychological wins faster.

I'd go avalanche if you can stick to it. You're saving real money in interest. But honestly, the best method is whichever one you'll actually follow through on, because the difference between a perfect strategy you abandon and a decent strategy you stick with is enormous.

The mechanics are simple. Pay the minimum on every card except the one you're targeting. Throw every extra dollar at that one card. When it's gone, take what you were paying on it and roll that full amount into the next card. Repeat.

Where people go wrong is treating the minimum payment as the goal. The minimum is designed to keep you paying interest for as long as possible. It's not a finish line, it's a trap. Even adding an extra $50 or $75 a month to your target card makes a significant difference when you run the numbers.

If your balance is large enough that the interest alone is eating most of your payment each month, it's worth calling your card issuer and asking about a hardship plan or a temporarily reduced rate. Most people don't do this because they assume the answer is no. Sometimes it isn't. One phone call costs you nothing.

## Finding the Money to Actually Pay It Down

This is where most advice gets vague and useless. "Cut back on spending" isn't a strategy. So let me be specific.

My first summer in New York I took home $3,800 a month, paid $2,100 in rent, and spent roughly $400 on groceries and transit. That doesn't leave a lot of room. But it completely reset how I think about where money actually goes versus where people assume it goes.

The places money leaks without feeling like it are almost always subscriptions and food. Not "going out" in some abstract sense, but specifically the $17.99 for a streaming service you watch twice a month, the $14 for Spotify when the free version exists, the $12.95 for an app you forgot you had. I'm not saying cancel everything. I'm saying audit it once, because most people are paying for things they've genuinely forgotten about.

On the income side, if you're a student, you probably have more options than you think. Tutoring on Wyzant pays $25 to $60 an hour depending on the subject and your level. Selling notes on Stuvia or Nexus Notes is passive once you've made them. If you have a campus job, check whether your hours can be increased. An extra $200 a month applied directly to your balance changes the payoff timeline significantly.

One more thing worth knowing: if you have a balance on a card with a high APR and you have decent credit, a balance transfer card might make sense. The Citi Simplicity card offers 0% intro APR for 21 months on balance transfers with a 3% transfer fee. On a $2,000 balance at 26% APR, that transfer fee costs you $60 upfront but saves you several hundred dollars in interest if you pay it down during the intro period. Just read the fine print before you do it. The rate that kicks in after the intro period is currently 19.24% to 29.99% variable, and you don't want to be sitting on a balance when that clock runs out.

## Keeping It From Happening Again

Paying off the balance matters. Understanding why it happened matters just as much.

For most people in college, credit card debt comes from one of two situations. Either you spent money you didn't have on things you wanted, or you spent money you didn't have on things you needed because something went wrong. Those are different problems with different solutions, and conflating them leads to bad advice.

If it was the first situation, the fix is behavioral. Building a simple budget, even just tracking where your money goes for one month, tends to be clarifying in a way that no amount of general advice is. I use a basic spreadsheet. Nothing fancy. It just forces me to be honest with myself about what I'm actually spending.

If it was the second situation, what you actually needed was an emergency fund, and you used a credit card because you didn't have one. Even $500 to $1,000 sitting in a high yield savings account changes everything when something goes wrong. My emergency fund sits in Marcus by Goldman Sachs, which is currently paying 4.10% APY with no minimum balance and no monthly fees. That's not going to make you rich, but it's money that's actually working for you instead of evaporating, and it's accessible when you need it.

Once the debt is gone and you've built even a small cushion, I'd encourage you to think about [improving your credit score](/posts/how-to-improve-your-credit-score-fast-in-college/) intentionally. Paying off debt and keeping utilization low are the two biggest levers you have right now. Using credit cards as a spending tool rather than a borrowing tool, meaning paying the full balance every month, is how you get the rewards without the interest. That's the whole game.

I've been running my Chase Freedom Flex as my daily driver and paying it in full every single month. No interest ever paid on it. The card earns 5% cash back on rotating quarterly categories, 3% on dining and drugstores, and 1% on everything else. The sign-up bonus is $200 after spending $500 in the first three months, and the annual fee is $0. That's a genuinely good deal, but only if you're not carrying a balance. The second you're paying 27% APR on purchases, no reward structure in existence makes it worth it.

Getting out of debt and keeping your credit score healthy are related goals, and it's worth understanding [what credit score you actually need for better cards](/posts/what-credit-score-do-you-need-for-a-student-credit-card/) as you build toward the next stage.

The goal isn't to fear credit cards. It's to use them correctly. And that starts with getting to zero.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Should I close my credit card after I pay it off?**
Probably not. Closing a card reduces your available credit, which increases your utilization ratio and can lower your score. Keep it open, use it occasionally for a small purchase, and pay it in full.

**Q: Does carrying a small balance help my credit score?**
No, this is a persistent myth. Paying your balance in full every month is better for your score than carrying a balance. The "carrying a small balance helps your credit" idea has no basis in how FICO scoring actually works.

**Q: Is a balance transfer card worth it for college students?**
It depends on your credit score and the size of your balance. You typically need a score of 670 or above to qualify for a good balance transfer offer. If you qualify and can realistically pay off the balance before the intro period ends, it can save you real money in interest.

**Q: What if I can't even make the minimum payment?**
Call your card issuer before you miss a payment, not after. Most issuers have hardship programs that can temporarily reduce your minimum payment or interest rate. A missed payment hurts your credit score significantly more than a phone call costs you in pride.

**Q: How long will it take to pay off my credit card debt?**
It depends on your balance, your APR, and how much you can pay each month. Use a free credit card payoff calculator, plug in your actual numbers, and you'll have a real timeline in about two minutes. Knowing the number is better than guessing.
