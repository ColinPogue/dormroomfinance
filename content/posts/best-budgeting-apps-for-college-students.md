I've tried more budgeting apps than I care to admit. Some I used seriously for months. Some I deleted after a week because they kept sending me notifications that made me feel like I was being graded. What I've learned is that the best budgeting app isn't the most popular one or the one with the cleanest UI. It's the one you'll actually open.

That said, not all of them are equal, and the differences matter depending on how you actually manage money. Here's what I've tested, what each one is genuinely good at, and where each falls short.

| App | Best For | Key Detail |
|---|---|---|
| YNAB | Zero-based budgeters who want control | $109/year, free for 12 months with student email |
| Rocket Money | Catching subscriptions and waste | Free tier available, Premium is $6 to $12/month |
| Copilot | iPhone users who want clean design | $13/month or $95/year, iOS only |
| Monarch Money | Couples or shared finances | $14.99/month or $99.99/year |
| Goodbudget | Cash envelope method, no bank sync needed | Free for 10 envelopes, Plus is $10/month |
| Mint Replacement (Credit Karma) | Basic free tracking | Free, limited budgeting depth |

## The Apps Worth Your Time

### YNAB (You Need a Budget)

YNAB operates on a specific philosophy: every dollar you have gets assigned a job before you spend it. You're not tracking what already happened, you're deciding what's going to happen. That distinction sounds small and it's actually massive.

I wrote a full breakdown of this one at [my YNAB review for college students](/posts/ynab-review-is-it-worth-it-for-college-students/) if you want the deep version. The short version is that YNAB costs $109 per year normally, but if you have a .edu email address you get 12 months free. That's not a limited trial. That's a full year of the actual product, no restrictions.

The learning curve is real. The first two weeks feel like homework. But the students I know who stuck with it past that point tend to have genuinely different relationships with their money, not just better spreadsheets.

### Rocket Money

Rocket Money is best at one specific thing: finding the places your money is quietly disappearing. It scans your connected accounts and flags recurring charges, then shows you exactly what you're paying for every month. I know people who found $60 to $80 in forgotten subscriptions within the first week.

The free tier exists but it's pretty limited for actual budgeting. Premium runs $6 to $12 per month depending on what you pay for the year, and it unlocks bill negotiation, credit score monitoring, and better analytics. I've got a full take on this one at my [Rocket Money review for college students](/posts/rocket-money-review-for-college-students-2026/) if you want more detail.

What Rocket Money isn't great at is forward planning. It's reactive by design. It tells you what happened. If you want to assign dollars before you spend them, YNAB is a better fit.

### Copilot

Copilot is an iPhone app and it's genuinely beautiful. That matters more than people admit because you're more likely to check something that feels good to use. It connects to your accounts, categorizes transactions automatically, and does a solid job of learning your habits over time.

At $13 per month or $95 per year, it's not cheap for a college student. It's also iOS only, which cuts out a significant chunk of people immediately. But if you're on iPhone, care about design, and want something that mostly runs itself with occasional check-ins from you, Copilot is probably the most pleasant day-to-day experience of anything on this list.

### Monarch Money

Monarch is built for people managing finances together, whether that's a couple, roommates who share expenses, or anyone splitting financial life with another person. The collaborative features are genuinely better than any competitor I've seen. Multiple users can see the same budget, add transactions, and comment on spending categories without it turning into a mess.

It costs $14.99 per month or $99.99 per year. That's real money for a college student, and I'd only recommend it if the shared budgeting piece is actually something you need. If you're flying solo on finances, there are better options at a lower price.

### Goodbudget

Goodbudget doesn't connect to your bank. You enter transactions manually. For most people that sounds like a dealbreaker and for most people it probably is. But there's a specific type of person who actually prefers this, because the act of manually recording a purchase makes you think about it in a way that automatic syncing doesn't.

It uses the cash envelope method, where you allocate money into named categories at the start of the month and spend against each envelope throughout. The free version gives you 10 envelopes, which is enough to cover the basics. Plus is $10 per month if you need more. At least in my experience, this one works best for people who've tried automatic-sync apps and found that disconnecting from the numbers too easily.

### Credit Karma (Mint Replacement)

When Mint shut down in 2024, a lot of people migrated to Credit Karma because it was the path of least resistance and it's free. Credit Karma is a fine credit monitoring tool. It's a mediocre budgeting tool. The budgeting features feel like an afterthought next to its core product, which is selling you credit cards and loans.

If you need something free and aren't ready to pay for anything else, it works as a basic transaction tracker. But don't expect it to actually change how you manage money. It mostly just shows you what you've already done.

## How to Actually Choose

The question that matters most isn't which app is best in general. It's what you actually need right now.

If you've never had a real budget and you want to build genuine habits, YNAB during the free student year is the strongest starting point on this list. The friction is the point. If your problem is that money leaks out without you knowing where it went, Rocket Money will show you the answer faster than anything else.

If you're already reasonably organized and want something that mostly runs in the background, Copilot does that well. And if you're managing money with a partner or roommates, Monarch is genuinely worth the premium.

I'd also say this: the app is not the strategy. I've met people running tight, intentional budgets in a Google Sheet and people with YNAB on their phone who haven't opened it in six months. The tool amplifies the intention. It doesn't replace it. If you want the underlying framework before you pick an app, my post on [how to budget in college on a tight budget](/posts/how-to-budget-in-college-on-a-tight-budget/) lays out the actual approach.

## A Few Things I'd Watch Out For

Free tiers are often designed to frustrate you into upgrading. That's not cynical, it's just the business model. Before you commit to a paid plan, run the free version long enough to know you'll actually use it. A $109 YNAB subscription you open twice is worse than a free spreadsheet you check every Sunday.

Also worth understanding: most of these apps make money by partnering with financial products and showing you offers. That doesn't make them bad, but it means some recommendations inside the app are ads in light clothing. Rocket Money and Credit Karma are the most obvious examples of this. I'm not saying ignore their suggestions, just read them with that context in mind.

One more thing. When my first internship paycheck hit and I realized $340 had been withheld in taxes I hadn't planned for, I had to completely recalculate what I had available to budget that month. No app would have caught that. Understanding the inputs, meaning your actual take-home number, matters as much as the software you use to track it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is YNAB actually free for college students?**
Yes. If you have a valid .edu email address, YNAB gives you 12 months free with no credit card required. After that it's $109 per year if you choose to continue.

**Q: What happened to Mint and what should I use instead?**
Mint shut down in early 2024. Credit Karma absorbed some of its users, but it's not a direct replacement for serious budgeting. YNAB, Rocket Money, and Copilot are stronger alternatives depending on what you need.

**Q: Do I need to connect my bank account to use these apps?**
Most of them work best with bank connections, but Goodbudget is specifically designed to work without one. If privacy or security is a concern, Goodbudget is the clearest option for manual-entry budgeting.

**Q: Can a budgeting app actually help if I'm living on very little money?**
Yes, though the benefit isn't magic. An app helps you see clearly where money is going and make deliberate choices. If you're working with a very tight number, my post on [how to live on $1,000 a month in college](/posts/how-to-live-on-1000-dollars-a-month-in-college/) is probably more useful as a starting point than any app.

**Q: Is Monarch Money worth it for roommates splitting expenses?**
If you're actively splitting finances and want shared visibility into a joint budget, yes. For casual expense splitting, a free app like Splitwise handles that better without the monthly cost.
