When I got my first credit card as a freshman, I had no idea there was even a distinction to make. I just knew I needed to start building credit. What I didn't know was that the type of card you start with actually matters, and that picking the wrong one can mean paying unnecessary fees or missing out on rewards you'd otherwise qualify for.

The short version: student credit cards are almost always the better starting point if you're enrolled in school and have at least some thin credit history. Secured cards exist for a reason, but that reason is usually not you.

| Feature | Secured Card | Student Credit Card |
|---|---|---|
| Credit required | None | Thin or limited okay |
| Security deposit | Yes, typically $200+ | No |
| Rewards | Rare | Common |
| Annual fee | Sometimes | Usually none |
| Best for | No credit at all | Students with any history |

## What Each Card Actually Is

### Secured Credit Cards

A secured card requires you to put down a cash deposit upfront, and that deposit becomes your credit limit. Put down $200, your limit is $200. The bank isn't taking a risk on you because they already have your money as collateral.

The Discover it Secured, for example, requires a minimum $200 deposit and has no annual fee. You earn 2% cash back at gas stations and restaurants up to $1,000 per quarter, and 1% on everything else. The APR is 28.24% variable, which is high, but it shouldn't matter if you're paying your balance in full every month. After seven months, Discover automatically reviews your account to see if you qualify to upgrade to an unsecured card and get your deposit back.

The Capital One Platinum Secured is another option. You can get a $200 credit limit with a deposit as low as $49 depending on your creditworthiness, and there's no annual fee. No rewards though, so you're purely using it as a credit building tool. That's fine if that's all you need, but there are usually better paths.

### Student Credit Cards

Student cards are unsecured, meaning no deposit required. They're designed for people in college who have limited credit history but haven't done anything to wreck their score either. Lenders know students are young and new to credit, so the approval criteria are more forgiving than a standard card.

My first card was the Discover it Student Cash Back. No annual fee, 5% cash back in rotating quarterly categories (think Amazon, restaurants, gas stations), and 1% on everything else. The APR is 18.24% to 27.24% variable depending on your credit. Discover also matches all the cash back you earn at the end of your first year, which is genuinely one of the better first year offers out there for a no annual fee card. I opened that card at 18 with basically no credit history and got approved without any trouble.

The Chase Freedom Student card is another solid pick. It's $20 annual credit for on-time payments, 1% cash back on everything, and a $50 bonus after your first purchase. Modest, but it reports to all three bureaus and it's a real card. You can read a full breakdown in the [Chase Freedom Student card review](/posts/chase-freedom-student-card-review/) if you want more detail on that one.

## When a Secured Card Actually Makes Sense

Here's where I want to be honest: there are real situations where a secured card is the right call.

If you have no credit history at all and you've already been denied for a student card, a secured card is a legitimate path forward. Some people come into college having never had any credit product, not even an authorized user account, and lenders won't touch them. A secured card gets you on the board. Six to twelve months of clean payment history on a secured card will usually get you to a place where you can qualify for something better.

If you've made some mistakes already, a secured card can help you rebuild. A missed payment or two at 18 can do real damage. I'd suggest reading [how credit scores work](/posts/how-credit-scores-work-explained-simply/) before you make any assumptions about where you stand, because a lot of people either overestimate or underestimate the impact of early mistakes. Secured cards don't care about your past the same way unsecured cards do, and that's exactly the point.

The main downside is opportunity cost. Your deposit is sitting there doing nothing when it could be in a Marcus by Goldman Sachs high yield savings account earning 4.1% APY. On a $300 deposit that's only $12 a year, not the end of the world, but still. And most secured cards don't offer meaningful rewards, so you're building credit without getting anything back for your spending.

## How to Actually Choose

If you're a current college student with no major credit blemishes, go apply for a student card first. Try the Discover it Student or the Bank of America Customized Cash Rewards for Students (3% in a category you choose, 2% at grocery stores and wholesale clubs, no annual fee, 28.24% variable APR). If you get approved, great. You're building credit and earning rewards at the same time.

If you get denied, that's useful information. It either means your credit history is too thin or there's something on your report you should look at. Check your report at AnnualCreditReport.com before applying anywhere. If the issue is just thin history, a secured card for six months to a year will usually fix it. There are also steps you can take to accelerate things, and [this guide on how to improve your credit score fast in college](/posts/how-to-improve-your-credit-score-fast-in-college/) covers the practical ones.

One thing I wish someone had told me earlier: the card you start with matters less than how you use it. I applied for the Chase Sapphire Preferred at about 14 months of credit history and got denied flat. Stung a little. But two years of clean payment history, a credit utilization rate under 10%, and a reapplication later I have the card I wanted. The fundamentals are what move the needle. The starting card is just the vehicle to build those fundamentals. If you want to understand [what credit score you need for a student credit card](/posts/what-credit-score-do-you-need-for-a-student-credit-card/) before applying, that's worth checking before you take an unnecessary hard inquiry.

One more thing: don't carry a balance on either of these cards. Both have high APRs. Secured cards typically run 25% to 29% variable. Student cards aren't much better. These are credit building tools, not financing tools. Pay them in full every month without exception and the APR is completely irrelevant to you.

Getting your first card approved and treating it right is genuinely one of the highest return-on-time financial moves you can make in your early twenties. A strong credit history means better apartment applications, lower car insurance in some states, and eventually access to cards and loan rates that actually benefit you. Starting at 18 or 19 with even a basic card means by the time you're looking at mortgages or a serious travel card you'll have almost a decade of history behind you. That compounding of credit history is underrated in the same way compound interest is underrated. People don't feel it until it suddenly matters a lot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can I get a student credit card without any credit history?**
Yes, most student cards are designed for people with limited or no credit history. Issuers like Discover and Bank of America explicitly market these cards to students who are just getting started.

**Q: Will a secured card hurt my credit score?**
Opening any new account causes a small temporary dip from the hard inquiry, but using a secured card responsibly will build your score over time. The net effect is strongly positive if you pay on time and keep your utilization low.

**Q: How long do I need to keep a secured card before upgrading?**
Most issuers will review your account after six to twelve months of on-time payments. Discover's secured card does an automatic review at seven months. You can also call your issuer and ask about a product change or deposit refund once you've built some history.

**Q: Can I have both a secured card and a student card at the same time?**
Yes, and in some cases it makes sense to keep both open even after upgrading, since closing accounts can shorten your average credit age. Just make sure both cards have no annual fee so there's no cost to keeping them open.

**Q: Does the security deposit on a secured card earn interest?**
It depends on the issuer, but usually no. Your deposit typically sits in a savings account or certificate of deposit held by the bank as collateral, and in most cases you don't see any interest on it. That's part of why you want to graduate off a secured card as soon as realistically possible.
