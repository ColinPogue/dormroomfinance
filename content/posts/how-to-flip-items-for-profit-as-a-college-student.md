I bought a used textbook for $18 at a campus sale my sophomore year, listed it on eBay two days later, and sold it for $74. That was about a $50 profit after fees and shipping, for maybe 45 minutes of work. It wasn't a life-changing amount of money, but it completely changed how I started looking at stuff around me.

Flipping items is one of the few side hustles that doesn't require a skill set you have to build from scratch, a portfolio, or a client. You need cash to start, a phone, and the ability to recognize when something is priced wrong. That's it. If you're also interested in service-based work, things like [freelancing on Upwork](/posts/how-to-make-money-on-upwork-as-a-college-student/) or [picking up dog walking gigs through Rover](/posts/rover-dog-walking-app-how-much-can-you-make/) can run alongside flipping without much overlap. But flipping has an edge the others don't: your time investment is mostly upfront, and then the listing just sits there working while you're in class.

Here's what I've actually learned doing this, not theory.

## What Actually Sells and Where to Find It

The biggest mistake beginners make is buying whatever they think is interesting and hoping someone else agrees. That's not a strategy. You want to buy things with proven, searchable demand and a consistent price history.

Textbooks are the obvious starting point if you're on a campus. The arbitrage is real because pricing is inconsistent across platforms. A book selling for $90 on the campus bookstore shelf might be listed for $20 at a library sale or a student Facebook group. Check the current eBay "sold" listings before you buy anything. Not the active listings — the sold ones. Active listings tell you what people are asking. Sold listings tell you what people actually paid.

Electronics are higher margin but also higher risk. A used iPad in good condition is worth real money, but you need to verify it's not iCloud locked before you hand over cash. That one mistake can turn a $180 potential profit into a $180 brick. Vintage cameras, especially 35mm film cameras from brands like Canon or Pentax, sell consistently right now. I've seen basic Canon AE-1s go for $120 to $180 on eBay depending on condition, and they show up at thrift stores for $15 to $40 regularly.

Clothing is where a lot of college flippers start, specifically branded streetwear and vintage pieces. Goodwill, Salvation Army, and local thrift chains like Savers are your sourcing ground. A Ralph Lauren polo in good condition goes for $25 to $45 on Depop. A vintage band tee from a name people actually search can go for $60 or more. The trick is learning what's valuable before you're standing in the aisle holding something, not while you're holding it. That knowledge takes a few weeks of browsing sold comps to build, but it builds fast.

Furniture is worth mentioning if you have access to a car or truck. College towns and cities around moving season, basically May and August, are gold mines. People leave solid wood dressers and bookshelves on curbs because they can't take them. A dresser that needs a sand and a coat of paint and sells for $280 on Facebook Marketplace is a real flip.

## The Math You Have to Know Before You List

Flipping looks more profitable than it is until you account for fees. This is where a lot of people get surprised.

eBay charges a final value fee of around 13.25% on most categories for sellers without a store subscription, plus a small fixed fee per order. Depop takes 10% plus PayPal processing fees if you're not using Depop Payments. Facebook Marketplace charges nothing for local cash transactions, which is why it's worth defaulting to local pickup for larger items when you can. Poshmark takes a flat $2.95 on sales under $15 and 20% on anything above that. Twenty percent is steep. Know that going in.

Shipping costs are the other thing that eats your margin if you're not careful. USPS Ground Advantage is your best option for most small to medium packages right now. A two pound item ships for roughly $8 to $11 depending on distance. If you're listing on eBay, use their built-in shipping calculator and select "calculated shipping" so the buyer's location determines the rate automatically. Don't guess at a flat rate and eat the difference.

My actual formula before I buy anything: expected sale price minus platform fees minus estimated shipping minus what I paid. If that number isn't at least $25 for something requiring significant time, or $15 for something quick and easy, I pass. That threshold keeps the hustle worth it.

## The Platforms Worth Your Time

You don't need to be everywhere. Pick two platforms and learn them well.

eBay is the broadest marketplace and the best for items with specific model numbers or ISBN numbers, so textbooks, electronics, cameras, and video games. The search volume is massive. If you're selling a Canon AE-1 or a specific edition of a finance textbook, there are buyers on eBay right now. The listing process takes about 10 minutes once you've done it a few times.

Depop and Poshmark both work for clothing, but they have different audiences. Depop skews younger and more streetwear focused. Poshmark skews slightly older and more toward mainstream brands. I use Depop for vintage and hype pieces, Poshmark for things like J.Crew, Lululemon, and mid-range branded basics. Photos matter a lot on both. A well-lit flat lay on a clean background will outsell a blurry mirror selfie of the same item every time.

Facebook Marketplace is underrated, especially for anything bulky or local. No fees on cash transactions, no shipping to deal with, and a huge buyer base in most cities. I use it almost exclusively for furniture and larger items. You do have to deal with flaky buyers sometimes, which is annoying but manageable.

Craigslist still works in certain markets for furniture and electronics. It's less polished than Marketplace but the buyer pool is real. In New York, I've sold things faster on Craigslist than Marketplace some weeks. It varies by city.

One thing I'd add: don't sleep on niche platforms if your inventory warrants it. Reverb is the best place to sell musical instruments. COMC and eBay both work for sports cards, though the sports card market has cooled significantly from its 2020 and 2021 peak, so manage expectations there.

## Turning This Into Something That Compounds

The actual goal isn't to sell one thing. It's to build a small, repeatable operation where you're sourcing, listing, and fulfilling consistently enough that income starts showing up on a weekly basis.

Start with whatever you already own that you don't use. List five things from your own room before you spend a dollar sourcing. This teaches you the platforms without any capital at risk. Once you've completed a few transactions and understand how shipping and fees work in practice, put $100 to $200 into your first sourcing run.

Track everything in a simple spreadsheet. What you paid, where you sourced it, platform fees, shipping cost, final sale price, and net profit. This sounds tedious and it is, slightly. But after two months you'll have real data on which categories are actually working for you, not just which ones feel like they're working.

The income from flipping is technically self-employment income once it crosses certain thresholds, so keep that in mind when tax season comes around. eBay and other platforms will send a 1099-K if you hit $5,000 in gross sales starting with the 2024 tax year under current IRS rules. Worth tracking your expenses throughout the year since your sourcing costs, shipping supplies, and mileage are all deductible against that income.

What you do with the profits matters as much as making them. My first internship paycheck had $340 withheld in taxes I hadn't accounted for. I spent an hour that night actually reading about FICA and federal withholding. Same energy applies here: understand what you're netting after taxes, then put a portion somewhere it does something. My Roth IRA at Fidelity accepts contributions from any earned income, and flipping income counts. Even $50 a month into FSKAX adds up faster than you'd expect when you're 21 and time is the actual asset.

Flipping isn't going to replace an internship salary, and it's not supposed to. But it's genuinely one of the better ways to generate a few hundred extra dollars a month without a fixed schedule, and the skill of spotting undervalued things is worth more than the money itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How much money do I need to start flipping items?**
You can start with zero by listing things you already own. If you want to source from thrift stores, $50 to $100 is enough to run a meaningful first sourcing trip without taking on real financial risk.

**Q: Do I have to pay taxes on money I make flipping?**
Yes. Profits from selling items are taxable income, and if you're consistently buying to resell, it's treated as self-employment income. Keep records of what you paid for items since that cost basis reduces your taxable profit.

**Q: What's the best platform for a beginner to start on?**
Facebook Marketplace for local cash sales and eBay for anything you're comfortable shipping. Both have large buyer bases and the fee structures are manageable once you understand them.

**Q: How do I know if something is worth buying to flip?**
Check the completed and sold listings on eBay before you buy. If the same item has sold multiple times recently at a price that leaves you a $25 or better margin after fees and shipping, it's worth considering.

**Q: Is flipping scalable enough to be a serious side income in college?**
Realistically, consistent flippers who source regularly can clear $300 to $600 a month in profit without it consuming their schedule. It's solid supplemental income, not a replacement for other opportunities like [freelance work on Fiverr](/posts/how-to-make-money-on-fiverr-as-a-student/) or a part-time job.

*I'm not a financial advisor, just a finance student sharing what I've actually done and learned. Do your own research before making any financial decisions.*
