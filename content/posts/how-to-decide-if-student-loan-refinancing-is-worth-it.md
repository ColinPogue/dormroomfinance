A refinance replaces existing debt with a new loan. Whether it is useful depends on the complete terms and the protections you would give up, not simply the advertised starting rate.

## Separate federal consolidation from private refinancing

Federal consolidation and private refinancing are different transactions. Moving federal loans into a private refinance removes federal protections attached to the old debt. Do not treat that as an administrative change you can simply undo later.

For private loans, compare the new agreement with the protections and payment flexibility you already have. A co-signer can also take on substantial responsibility; understand any release conditions.

## Compare a like-for-like scenario

Request the actual rate and term offered to you, including fees, fixed or variable status, payment amount, and total repayment cost. Advertising usually does not establish what you personally qualify for.

Keep the repayment period comparable. A longer term can lower the monthly payment while increasing interest paid overall. A variable rate can change, so consider whether future payments remain affordable.

## Decide what the lost flexibility is worth

Consider income uncertainty, possible federal repayment or discharge options, and how much cash you have available if work stops. A modest rate reduction may not compensate for losing important protections.

If the tradeoff is unclear, pause and obtain current guidance before signing. An application or quote is not a requirement to accept a new loan.
