I'm not a financial advisor, just a finance student sharing what I've actually done and learned. Do your own research before making any financial decisions.

The question of whether to throw extra money at student loans while you're still in school sounds simple. It's not. The answer depends on what kind of loans you have, what interest rate you're carrying, and what else you could do with that money instead. Getting the first two wrong means the third one doesn't even matter.

Here's how I actually think about this, and what I'd tell a friend who asked me over coffee.

## The Interest Accrual Problem Most People Ignore

If you have unsubsidized federal loans, interest starts accruing from the day you borrow. Not from graduation. Not from when repayment starts. Right now.

The standard federal unsubsidized rate for undergrads in the 2025-2026 academic year is 6.53%. On a $10,000 loan, that's roughly $653 in interest building up every year you're in school. If you're a freshman borrowing that amount, by the time you graduate four years later you're looking at somewhere around $2,800 in accrued interest added to your principal before you've made a single required payment. That matters because your repayment calculation is based on the new, higher balance.

Subsidized loans are a different story. The government covers the interest while you're enrolled at least half-time, so there's no runaway compounding happening in the background. If all your loans are subsidized, the urgency to pay while in school drops significantly. You're not fighting a ticking clock the same way.

If you're not sure which type you have, log into studentaid.gov and check your loan breakdown before you do anything else. I'd also point you to this breakdown of [federal vs private student loans](/posts/federal-vs-private-student-loans-differences/) if you're fuzzy on the structural differences, because they affect this decision more than most people realize.

## Paying Interest While in School: Does It Actually Help?

Technically, yes. Meaningfully, it depends on how much you can put toward it.

If you're earning money through a part-time job or an internship, you have the option to make interest-only payments while you're still enrolled. This keeps your balance from growing during the in-school deferment period. It doesn't reduce what you owe, but it prevents compounding from quietly adding hundreds of dollars to your total balance.

My first internship paycheck had $340 withheld in taxes I hadn't accounted for. I'd built a rough spending plan based on gross pay and had to recalibrate everything. That experience taught me to be realistic about what's actually available to work with before committing to any extra financial obligation, including voluntary loan payments.

If after taxes, rent, food, and a reasonable buffer you have $200 a month sitting there, throwing it at loan interest on a 6.53% unsubsidized loan is a solid move. You're earning an effective 6.53% return on that money by avoiding interest, and that's better than most savings accounts. Marcus by Goldman Sachs is currently paying 4.10% APY on their high yield savings account, which is good. It's not 6.53%.

But here's where it gets more complicated.

## The Roth IRA Argument You Should Take Seriously

There is a legitimate case that contributing to a Roth IRA while you're in college beats making early loan payments, and I genuinely believe it for a specific type of student in a specific situation.

The Roth IRA contribution limit is $7,000 for 2025. You can only contribute up to your earned income for the year, so if you made $4,000 from a summer job, that's your ceiling. The key advantage is that contributions grow tax-free, and the years you contribute in your early 20s are worth more than almost any other years because of the compounding timeline. A dollar invested at 20 has roughly 45 years to grow before traditional retirement age. A dollar invested at 30 has 35. That gap is significant.

I opened my Roth IRA at 19 with $400 from my first paycheck that actually felt like real money. Set it up at Fidelity, put it into FSKAX which is their total market index fund with a 0% expense ratio, and the whole thing took maybe 20 minutes. It was completely anticlimactic. That was the point.

FSKAX has no minimum investment, no transaction fees, and a 0% expense ratio. There is no cheaper way to get diversified exposure to the entire U.S. equity market. For a college student with limited capital, that matters.

So the actual comparison is: pay down a 6.53% loan early, or invest in a Roth IRA where your long-run expected return in a broad index fund is somewhere around 7% to 10% annually over decades. The numbers are close enough that the Roth often wins when you factor in the tax-free growth and the irreplaceable value of those early contribution years.

The exception is private student loans. If you borrowed privately and you're sitting on a 10% or 12% interest rate, the math flips. Paying down guaranteed high-interest debt beats speculative investment returns every time. No debate there.

## How to Actually Make the Decision

Stop trying to find the universally correct answer. It doesn't exist. The right move depends on three things: your loan type, your interest rate, and what else you can realistically do with the money.

If you have only subsidized federal loans at 6.53% or lower, there's a reasonable argument for letting them sit during school, building an emergency fund, and prioritizing Roth IRA contributions up to your earned income limit. The interest isn't compounding against you during enrollment and your early investment years have enormous long-term value.

If you have unsubsidized federal loans, consider at minimum making interest-only payments during school. It's not glamorous but it prevents your balance from ballooning before you even start working full time.

If you have private loans at a high variable rate, paying those down aggressively while you're earning anything is probably the right call. And if you've already graduated and you're revisiting this, you should read about [how to pay off student loans fast after graduation](/posts/how-to-pay-off-student-loans-fast-after-graduation/) because the in-school calculus changes once repayment actually kicks in.

One thing I'd push back on: the idea that any extra money you have should automatically go to debt. Liquidity matters when you're 20 and income is inconsistent. Having three months of expenses in a high yield savings account like Marcus, pulling 4.10% APY with no minimum balance requirement, gives you flexibility that an accelerated loan payoff doesn't. If your car dies or you lose your campus job, cash on hand keeps you from putting emergencies on a credit card at 20% APR.

Build your emergency fund first. Then decide between early loan payments and Roth contributions based on your actual interest rates. That ordering matters more than most people give it credit for.

The honest summary is that paying off student loans while in school can absolutely be the right move, but it's rarely the only right move and sometimes it's not even the best one. Run your actual numbers, know what type of loans you're carrying, and make a deliberate choice rather than a reflexive one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Does paying interest on student loans while in school actually make a difference?**
Yes, especially for unsubsidized loans. Paying interest during the in-school deferment period prevents it from capitalizing into your principal, which keeps your total repayment balance lower from day one.

**Q: Should I prioritize a Roth IRA or early student loan payments?**
If your loan interest rate is below roughly 7%, there's a reasonable case for prioritizing Roth contributions, particularly in your early 20s when compounding time is most valuable. If your rate is above that, paying down the debt first usually wins.

**Q: Can I make loan payments while still enrolled in school?**
Yes. There's no requirement to wait until after graduation. You can make interest-only or even principal payments at any point, and your loan servicer will apply them immediately.

**Q: What if I can only afford to do one thing at a time?**
Build a small emergency fund of at least one to two months of expenses first, then decide between early loan payments and Roth contributions based on your specific interest rate. Skipping the emergency fund to pay down debt faster often backfires.

**Q: Does paying off loans early hurt my credit score?**
Closing a loan account in good standing can slightly reduce your credit age and mix, but the effect is usually minor and the interest savings typically outweigh any short-term score impact.
